# Word Choice

A word-level pass to run after the main taste pass, not instead of it. Taste
governs sentences, paragraphs, and structure. This governs individual words —
the ones that quietly change your tone.

The premise: language is a free way to be strategic. In text you have no face, no
voice, no timing, so a word that reads as neutral out loud reads as cold, unsure,
or negative on the page. The right words make you sound warm, certain, and in
control. The wrong ones make you sound passive, defensive, or confrontational —
even when you're a team player in real life. Don't shoot yourself in the foot
with language that diminishes your own intent and authority.

Run this as a `ctrl-F` sweep. For each word below, find it and decide: cut it, or
swap it. Most of the time you cut.

## Tone leaks — words that read colder or more negative than you mean

Neutral or even polite out loud. On the page they chill the room. This is the
pass most people skip.

- **fine** — "fine" in text reads as *not* fine. You have no tone of voice to
  carry the warmth, so amp up the positivity to compensate.
  - 🚫 "I'm fine with that." → 👍 "That sounds good." / "Great, I'm on board."
- **however** — a melodramatic, formal way to say "but." It makes ideas sound
  more negative than they have to be and skews corporate. If you need a negating
  word, use "but" — or restructure so you don't need a pivot at all.
  - 🚫 "...on your schedule. However, we'll record these..." → 👍 "...on your own
    schedule. For your convenience, we'll record these so you can watch on
    demand at your leisure."
- **unfortunately** — makes things sound worse than they are, and subconsciously
  accepts blame. If you offer something on set terms, you don't owe an apology
  for it. Phrase neutral news neutrally. If you must negate, use "but."
  - 🚫 "Unfortunately, we only offer the sessions on X dates, but..." → 👍
    "Absolutely. The sessions are scheduled on X dates, but..."
- **no problem** — when you say it about work that actually took real effort, you
  diminish the care you put in. Like deflecting a compliment because it feels
  awkward. Accept appreciation graciously; it makes both people feel good.
  - 🚫 "Oh, it was no problem." → 👍 "My pleasure. Glad it was helpful."

**Tip:** end sentences on a positive word. The reader's eye lands on the first
and last words more than the middle. "...at your leisure" beats "...no big deal."

## Say it in the affirmative — framings that make the reader work

State what *is*, and what you *can* do. Negation forces the reader to picture the
thing, then flip it.

- **double negatives** — even the ones that aren't strictly double negatives make
  the reader decode a "not." State it in the affirmative as the default; break
  the rule only on purpose. Watch "only" too — it implies scarcity when you mean
  abundance.
  - 🚫 "He's not wrong." → 👍 "He has a point."
  - 🚫 "I could not be more proud to..." → 👍 "I am incredibly proud to..."
  - 🚫 "I have nothing but positive things to say." → 👍 "I have so many positive
    things to say."
  - (Short, idiomatic ones are fine: "I couldn't have said it better." The longer
    the sentence, the more a double negative costs.)
- **can't until** — states a concession and gives up your power. Don't lead with
  what you can't do; lead with what you can.
  - 🚫 "I can't meet until 2pm." → 👍 "I can meet at 2pm and after."

## Authority-undercutters — words that shrink you

- **just** — your sentence is stronger without it. Delete it and notice how much
  more confident you sound, with no loss of meaning.
  - 🚫 "I just wanted to ask..." → 👍 "I wanted to ask..."
- **honestly / to be honest** — assume telling the truth is your default. Flagging
  your honesty implies you're usually not honest.
  - 🚫 "To be honest, I don't think that's a good idea." → 👍 "I don't think
    that's a good idea."
- Other hedges in the same family, cut or state directly: **I think / I feel
  like**, **maybe / perhaps / kind of / sort of**, **"I'm no expert, but,"**
  **"quick question."** Keep a hedge only when you're flagging real uncertainty
  on purpose.

## Dead weight — intensifiers and filler that weaken

- **truly / very / really** — you come across as *more* sincere without them.
  Stacking emphasizers dilutes them; "truly" in particular can sound melodramatic.
  Use sparingly and on purpose (a single well-placed "really" can add emphasis).
  - 🚫 "I really appreciate you and I'm truly glad to be here." → 👍 "I appreciate
    you and I'm honored to be here."
  - **Level up:** don't just delete — swap for a more evocative word. "truly
    glad" → "honored." "I was truly excited" → "I was jumping out of my seat."
- Also cut on sight: **basically, literally, obviously, actually** (reads as
  corrective), **in order to** → "to", **the fact that** → "that", **at this
  point in time** → "now", **currently / at the moment** (the present tense
  already says it).

## The test

Read the sentence with the word and without it. If the meaning survives the cut,
the word was filler — delete it. If the tone warms up when you swap it, the
original was leaking. Keep only the words that earn their place.

## How this relates to taste

`taste` bans hedging and AI transitions as a category. This skill is the fast,
concrete checklist for enforcing that by hand on a short piece — the exact words
to search for, with swaps. A full `/edit` pass already covers this. Reach for
`word-choice` on its own when you want a three-minute cleanup on an email, Slack
message, or update, not a full rewrite.
