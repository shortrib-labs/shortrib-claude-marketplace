# Draft Patterns

Two patterns to check after the main taste pass, not instead of it.

## AI Parenthetical Construct

The most common AI writing pattern isn't the em dash — it's the structure behind it: a claim immediately followed by its negation or qualifier.

**The construct:** "X, not just Y." / "Not X, but Y." / "The point isn't X; it's Y."

Swapping the em dash for a comma or period leaves the construct intact. The fix is to rewrite so the sentence leads with the affirmative claim. The negation disappears because the positive version says it directly.

**Test:** Can each sentence stand alone without an immediate qualifier or negation? If every claim requires a parenthetical to say what it doesn't mean, the writing is structured around absences rather than presences.

**Example fix:** "The answer names specific actions, not general presence" → "The answer describes what the AE did."

## Parenthetical Insertion

A related but distinct problem: claims qualified by mid-sentence insertions — double em-dash asides, colon-dumps, `(X, Y, Z)` lists dropped into a sentence. These survive the negation/contrast fix because they don't use "not X" framing. Instead, they over-qualify by adding a bracketed elaboration that should be its own sentence.

The deeper problem is rhythm. When a sentence starts strong and then stalls for an insertion, the reader has to hold the subject in suspension, process the aside, and reconnect. A sentence can be long and still move — good prose builds, the information arrives in a direction. AI sentences tend to be long because they stop and restart. The parenthetical inverts forward pull: the sentence starts, stalls, then tries to continue. Over-qualification is the symptom; broken momentum is the problem.

**Signs:** A sentence that requires a parenthetical to be complete. A colon followed by a list that isn't actually a list.

**Fix:** Remove the parenthetical. Ask whether the sentence still says what needs to be said. If yes, cut it. If not, promote it to its own sentence.

Run this as a second targeted pass after the negation/contrast pass when the piece is second-draft or later — catching one doesn't catch the other.

## Draft Maturity Framework

Where is this piece in its development? The answer changes what kind of editing is useful.

### First Draft Signals
- Inconsistent POV: voice shifts between first and second person, or "from my perspective" vs. "as the developer" within the same piece
- Hedging that signals the writer's uncertainty, not the subject's complexity: "I have to admit," "one might argue," "it should be noted"
- Placeholder sections: comments like `<!-- explain why... -->`, headers with no content
- Facts present but not yet in the right order — thinking on the page rather than structured for a reader
- Flat openings: chronological starts, "In this post I will..."

### Second Draft Signals
- Structure settled — the sections are right even if the prose needs work
- Argument visible, ideas clear
- Voice inconsistent but present: some strong passages, some still rough
- Obvious line-level repairs needed

### Near-Final Signals
- Voice consistent throughout
- Structure clean — each section earns its place
- Needs a line-level pass, not structural rethinking

Good revision is progressive: voice consistency first, then structure, then cut.
